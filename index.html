Why Under pressure

The world's forests are under pressure.

Fire, drought, heat and clearing, on every forested continent. The most extreme wildfires are 2.2 times as frequent as in 2003,1 drought and heat are killing trees worldwide,2 and 10.9 million hectares of forest are still cleared each year.3

Why Too slow

We can simulate forests from theory. Slowly.

Process-based models such as the Ecosystem Demography model (ED) grow every forest from established ecology, and their results inform the Global Carbon Budget.20,21,22 But one 40-year global run takes ED 40.6 days on 20 CPU cores, and finer maps and more futures multiply that.17

Why Emulators

We need emulators: fast, accurate, knowledge-grounded.

Trained on a process-based model's own runs, an AI emulator learns to reproduce the theory, and runs in about an hour. Building one takes those runs, ready for machine learning. That is CarbonGlobe.

Primer Inside ED

One forest. Four forcings. Fifteen ages.

The Ecosystem Demography model (ED) grows each forest year by year from monthly climate, CO₂ and soil.20,21 Different forcings give different fates, here a wet start, the 2015 drought,6 a sketched fire and the boreal cold, and every site starts from fifteen forest ages, 1 to 500 years old.

Primer From space

Real forests, measured from orbit.

Since 2019, NASA's GEDI lidar on the International Space Station has measured the height and structure of forests in 25 m footprints between 51.6°N and 51.6°S.24 ED is calibrated and evaluated against satellite lidar, forest inventories and carbon fluxes.21,25,26

FAQ

Common questions.

Why not just run ED?

ED stays the reference. But one 40-year global run at 0.5° takes 40.6 days on 20 CPU cores, a run at 0.1° would take about 25 times longer, and every new climate scenario or starting forest is another run.17 An emulator trained on CarbonGlobe does each run in about an hour, so finer maps and many more futures become affordable.

What is an AI emulator?

A neural network trained to reproduce a simulator: the same inputs in, the same outputs out, orders of magnitude faster.

How does CarbonGlobe relate to ERA5 and WeatherBench 2?

Its climate inputs are the same kind of gridded reanalysis (here NASA's MERRA-2). WeatherBench 230 asks a model to predict the atmosphere; CarbonGlobe asks what forests do with it. It is the land-carbon counterpart that can sit next to AI weather and climate models.

Is it observations or simulation?

The inputs are observation-based products. The targets are simulations from ED, a process model calibrated and evaluated against lidar, flux towers, forest inventories and atmospheric inversions, whose results feed the Global Carbon Budget.22,28 An emulator trained on CarbonGlobe learns ED.

How big is it, and what hardware do I need?

29.3 GB of float32 NumPy arrays. Memory-map them and stream batches, as in the quick start. The paper's timings for global inference come from a single RTX A4500 GPU.17

Does it cover future climates?

The dataset spans 1981–2021, so ED could be calibrated against real observations. The IEEE BigData Cup 2026 challenge adds two future scenarios, SSP1-2.6 and SSP5-8.5, and CMIP6-driven runs to 2100 are planned.

Can I use it in my own work?

Yes. Data and code are released under CC BY 4.0: use, share and adapt them with attribution. Please cite the NeurIPS 2025 paper.

Read the abstract

Forest ecosystems play a critical role in the Earth system as major carbon sinks that are essential for carbon neutralization and climate change mitigation. However, the Earth has undergone significant deforestation and forest degradation, and the remaining forested areas are also facing increasing pressures from socioeconomic factors and climate change, potentially pushing them towards tipping points. Responding to the grand challenge, a theory-based Ecosystem Demography (ED) model has been continuously developed over the past two decades and serves as a key component in major initiatives, including the Global Carbon Budget, NASA Carbon Monitoring System, and US Greenhouse Gas Center. Despite its growing importance in combating climate change and shaping carbon policies, ED's expensive computation significantly limits its ability to estimate carbon dynamics at the global scale with high spatial resolution. Recently, machine learning (ML) models have shown promising potential in approximating theory-based models with interesting success in various domains including weather forecasting, thanks to the open-source benchmark datasets made available. However, there are currently no publicly available ML-ready datasets for global carbon dynamics forecasting in forest ecosystems. The limited data availability hinders the development of corresponding ML emulators. Furthermore, the inputs needed for running ED are highly complex with over a hundred variables from various remote sensing products. To bridge the gap, we develop a new ML-ready benchmark dataset, CarbonGlobe, for carbon dynamics forecasting, featuring that: (1) the data has a global-scale coverage at 0.5° resolution; (2) the temporal range spans 40 years; (3) the inputs integrate extensive multi-source data from different sensing products, with calibrated outputs from ED; (4) the data is formatted in ML-ready forms and split into different evaluation scenarios based on climate conditions, etc.; (5) a set of problem-driven metrics is designed to develop benchmarks using various ML models to best align with the needs of downstream applications.
